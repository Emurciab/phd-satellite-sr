Stormwater Treatment Areas (STAs) are engineered wetlands built to reduce phosphorus (P) loads to the Florida Everglades, but despite retaining more than 3,400 tons of total P over 24 years, they have not consistently achieved the strict Water Quality Based Effluent Limit (WQBEL). Mechanistic models have been developed to represent P cycling across pools, ecosystem components, and processes, and have benefited from long-term monitoring, but their accuracy and transferability to other STAs depend on how well parameters are constrained. Current approaches require recalibration to correct errors, particularly for vegetation-associated parameters, and lack efficient ways to incorporate spatial heterogeneity or observational data into parameterization. At the same time, consistent data collection on aquatic vegetation remains difficult due to the large extent of STAs, costs and limitations of field and drone surveys, and the insufficient spatial or temporal resolution of open-source satellite imagery. While commercial providers offer higher resolution products, budget constraints limit their use, and single-source monitoring strategies have proven inadequate. As a result, datasets that provide spatio-temporally consistent information on vegetation metrics needed for model calibration are still missing. This research addresses these gaps by developing a remote-sensing–informed framework that integrates super-resolution to upsample frequent lower-resolution imagery, observation models to map RS features to vegetation state variables, causal discovery to identify connections among hydrologic, water quality, and vegetation features, and a multi-objective probabilistic calibration routine. The central hypothesis is that remote sensing data can be transformed to correct model state trajectories and support probabilistic calibration, improving parameter estimation, reducing error compensation, and enhancing model transferability and uncertainty quantification across STAs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background and Research Gap}\label{sec:Background_and_Research Gap}
The background and research gap from this research have been incorporated into a manuscript that is in the process of being submitted to a Journal. A draft copy of it can be found in this
\href{https://www.overleaf.com/read/sydzfrtjyxgg#5e0ef2}{link}.

\newpage

Stormwater Treatment Areas (STAs) are engineered tropical wetlands designed to help restore the oligotrophic conditions of the Florida Everglades Protection Area, which were degraded by decades of agricultural and urban discharges. Over the last 31 years, the STAs have retained more than 3,721 metric tons of total phosphorus (TP), reduced the TP load by 78\%, and enabled outflow TP flow-weighted mean concentrations (FWMC) of at least 25 $\mu$g/L. STAs retain P through biological uptake by macrophytes and periphyton, particle settling, soil accretion, and co-precipitation driven by chemical reactions \citep{influencebhomia2018}. The SFWMD continues to deploy management and modeling efforts to further reduce TP concentrations (STA-1E, -1W, -2, and -3/4, are currently at $20\mu$g/L or less) to meet the Water Quality Based Effluent Limit (WQBEL) (i.e., an annual flow-weighted mean of $13,\mu$g/L in more than three out of five consecutive water years) and to evaluate the effects of environmental change and management strategies \citep{SFWMD2024_modelreport}.

Research has focused on developing modeling frameworks that represent the physical, chemical, and biological processes driving P cycling under ultra-low concentrations (i.e., $<20,\mu$g/L), leverage historical system data to capture trends and patterns, and remain computationally tractable \citep{LARIOS2024107131,datadrivenjuston2019,spatiotemporalpaudel2011,SFWMD2024_modelreport}. For instance, the most recent model, co-developed by the SFWMD, was conceptualized by disaggregating P cycling across pools (dissolved and particulate; organic and inorganic), ecosystem components (macrophytes, periphyton, floc, soil, water), and processes (deposition, uptake, precipitation, re-suspension, hydrolysis, diffusion), represented as state variables and process rate equations. Long-term monitoring data collected from STA-2 FW-1 were used for model parameterization, calibration, and validation.

While these frameworks have advanced process representation and benefited from long-term monitoring, the accuracy and generalization of model results to other STAs ultimately depend on how well parameters are constrained while acknowledging that in complex systems, multiple parameter sets can produce acceptable outputs due to compensatory interactions (i.e., equifinality) \citep{JUSTON2013135,BEVEN200111}. This is illustrated in \citet{SFWMD2024_modelreport}, where parameters were adjusted to correct large overestimations of particulate organic P. These recalibration needs underscore the importance of carefully selecting parameters that govern the dynamics of the state variables within the model. In addition, parameterization should account for spatial variability within the system, while preventing excessive local parameterization that would increase equifinality issues and make the model descriptive rather than predictive. For the STAs, the SFWMD addressed spatial structure in the bioenergetics-based food-web/ecosystem model (CASM) by simulating multiple segments with segment-specific states and drivers; core producer parameter values are specified globally across segments. Granted an optimal aggregation scale, these models could benefit from observation-informed parameterization and data assimilation to correct trajectories in the current specification of state variables, accounting for the phenological and spatial heterogeneity of vegetation states, as well as disturbances that are not explicitly represented in the current model, and to establish efficient multi-objective routines for estimating parameters in the calibration stage. This would help reduce compensating errors carried over to other state variables (e.g., biotic variables) and improve model transferability to other STAs, which is necessary given the marked differences among STAs in performance, biogeochemistry, hydrology, soils, inflow water, weather, configuration, and vegetation composition.

However, consistent data collection from aquatic vegetation communities that could be used to support model parameterization, calibration, and validation across the STAs remains difficult due to a combination of reasons, including large areas of study; high demands on spatial and spectral resolution to map temporal changes in phenological features or model metrics such as biomass and carbon storage; atmospheric and water-background interference on optical sensors; similar backscattering among species or with open water for radar sensors; difficult access to the inner sections of the STAs; temporal-scale mismatches among multiple data sources; and budget constraints.

Field surveys are necessary but insufficient for monitoring the spatiotemporal changes of aquatic vegetation species, as they are labor-intensive and can only be deployed at specific points within the wetlands. Drone monitoring has been considered a complementary system in the STAs, but the costs of consistently flying drones at medium and large scales (especially when using hyperspectral or LiDAR sensors) have been limiting. Satellite imagery-based monitoring has been used at larger scales; however, the spatial resolution and temporal availability of open-source imagery have limited its use in obtaining the data consistency needed to compile inputs for modeling and management purposes \citep{applyingzhang2017}. Commercial satellite providers (WorldView, PlanetScope, Pleiades) offer optical imagery with the spatial detail and revisit that help in transition zones and for small features, and newer sensors bring useful red-edge bands \citep{comparison_vandeventer_2022,mapping_rupasinghe_2021,modeling_belloli_2025,advancing_hemati_2025}; however, cost remains a barrier. Radar data, useful for overcoming atmospheric interference affecting optical sensors, has limited applicability due to costs (for commercial sources), spatial or temporal coverage (for open-source sources), minimum order quantity, and processing requirements \citep{rsSAR}.

As technical and economic constraints limit the use of single-source strategies for aquatic vegetation monitoring in constructed wetland systems, researchers and managers have adopted a mix of approaches, including sporadic drone surveys, field visits, open-source and commercial satellite imagery (optical and radar), LiDAR, and helicopter surveys. These efforts have helped address several management needs, including maintaining species inventories via classification maps, controlling undesired growth or die-offs, and detecting floating islands. However, for model-oriented data collection, there is still a need for datasets that provide the required information at appropriate spatial and temporal resolutions.

To meet this need, a multi-source framework that delivers data for key model metrics (e.g., biomass, carbon, and nutrient storage) at spatial and temporal resolutions that match the pace of phenological change in target aquatic-vegetation species, and that remains accessible given the budget constraints common in constructed wetland systems, is needed. Such a framework would provide data to constrain parameter estimates for calibrating wetland biogeochemical models (including the model co-developed by the SFWMD) and for conducting formal global sensitivity analysis and uncertainty analysis. By linking observational data with model parameters and propagating measurement uncertainty, calibration can reduce reliance on testing discrete grids of parameter values across predefined ranges and on one-at-a-time sensitivity analyses. This, in turn, can improve transferability to other STAs and how the model represents phosphorus cycling outside the original calibration site.

\subsection{Main Objective and Central Hypothesis} \label{sec:Specific_Objectives}

The main objective of this research is to develop and test a remote-sensing–informed parameterization, data assimilation, and probabilistic calibration framework for aquatic vegetation state variables and process-terms in constructed wetland biogeochemical models. We will use the BGC and CASM models co-developed by the SFWMD. The framework will leverage instances of high spatial resolution imagery to train larger sets of lower spatial resolution, solving the limited temporal availability of the former when budget restrictions exist, and creating the necessary time series data to inform the parameterization of the models. Then, observation operators will be defined to map RS features (e.g., fractional cover, leaf area index, spectral indices) to model variables (e.g., biomass carbon, photosynthetic rate) and then aggregated to pre-defined spatial units (e.g., flow-ways). This will produce the necessary time series for data assimilation. The aggregates will then be incorporated into the existing models either as time- and segment-dependent scalars (of the initial parameters) or nudging terms (to correct trajectories on the state's ODE) in the equations where aquatic vegetation state variables were defined in the BGC and CASM models. For calibration, we will implement a multi-objective probabilistic approach using water-quality time series and vegetation time series targets that will reduce reliance on grid searches and provide uncertainty estimation. For this, we will use causal discovery as a tool to inform prior selection of the probabilistic calibration

My central hypothesis is that remote sensing data can be transformed and used to correct trajectories (nudge) of state variables in mechanistic biogeochemical and hydrological models, and to develop formal probabilistic calibration routines that can help improve the estimation of model parameters. This will be essential to reduce error compensation among the biotic and abiotic state variables defined in the models, increase model transferability to other STAs/flow-ways, and have formal uncertainty estimates from the models' outputs. The proposed methodology can help remove some accessibility barriers that end users with limited budgets face, and increase the value of RS products that have been collected unsystematically, to produce a reproducible methodology to guide the model parameterization and calibration while respecting the mechanistic principles of the models.

\subsection{Specific Objective 1}

Create dense time series of high-resolution and low-cost RS imagery from the STAs. My central hypothesis is that a super-resolution-based framework can deliver higher-detail, higher-frequency estimates needed to inform biogeochemical models about key vegetation dynamics, without continuous high-resolution acquisitions (i.e., UAV, commercial satellite imagery), while preserving spectral fidelity.

\subsubsection{Methodology}

The study area includes all five STAs (STA-1E, STA-1W, STA-2, STA-3/4, and STA-5/6). All STAs were included to maximize data availability for model training and to ensure that the SR framework generalizes across sites with different vegetation composition, hydrology, and geometry.

\paragraph{\textbf{Stage 1 — Sentinel-2 / PlanetScope SR (Completed)}}

\subparagraph{Data acquisition and preprocessing.}
PlanetScope SuperDove scenes (3~m resolution; five spectral bands: Blue, Green, Red, NIR, Red~Edge) were ingested into a Google Earth Engine (GEE) repository via Planet's Education and Research Program. For each site-year combination, a composite quality score combining the cloud/shadow fraction derived from the UDM2 mask (confidence threshold $\geq 80\%$), the solar-zenith-angle proximity to solar noon, and acquisition timestamp as a tie-breaker was used to select the best-quality daily mosaic per date. Each PlanetScope date was paired to a Sentinel-2 SR Harmonized (\texttt{COPERNICUS/S2\_SR\_HARMONIZED}) acquisition within a $\pm7$-day tolerance using nearest-neighbour date matching, retaining only dates with an area-of-interest cloud fraction below 10\% in both sensors. Image co-registration was performed using AROSICS \citep{scheffler2017}, which estimates and applies local, sub-pixel polynomial shifts aligning the PlanetScope image (HR) to the Sentinel-2 image (LR reference). Spectral harmonization was applied band-wise via histogram matching, transferring the PlanetScope reflectance distribution towards that of Sentinel-2, resulting in a 99.9\% reduction in inter-sensor spectral mismatch. All processing was implemented server-side in GEE where possible and downloaded in parallelized chunks (up to 12 concurrent workers on a $9 \times 9$ spatial grid) to minimize transfer time and memory demands.

Registered pairs were tiled in a non-overlapping spatial grid, producing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HR target}: $256 \times 256$ pixel tiles at 3~m (PlanetScope, 3-band: R, G, NIR as the core training target bands matching Sentinel-2 10~m equivalents, plus the 20~m-equivalent Red~Edge band where required).
    \item \textbf{LR input (10~m)}: $77 \times 77$ pixel Sentinel-2 tiles (3 bands: B, G, R).
    \item \textbf{LR input (20~m)}: $38 \times 38$ pixel Sentinel-2 tiles (2 bands: NIR, Red~Edge).
\end{itemize}

Each sample consists of a temporal sequence of three consecutive cloud-acceptable acquisition dates within the same year, providing the multi-frame context required for temporal attention. The final dataset contains 6,839 temporal sequences (5,472 training; 1,367 test; 80/20 stratified split by site-year), with contributions from all five STAs across years 2022–2024 (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats}).

\subparagraph{Model architecture.}
A novel multi-resolution, multi-output temporal attention SR network was designed to jointly super-resolve the 10~m and 20~m Sentinel-2 bands to 3~m resolution (scale factor ${\approx}3.33\times$). The network comprises four main modules:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Per-frame encoder}: Depthwise separable convolutions with GroupNorm and GELU activations; CBAM (Convolutional Block Attention Module) spatial–channel attention inserted every two residual blocks.
    \item \textbf{Temporal cross-attention module}: Per-frame global-pooling embeddings transformed by a shared MLP; softmax-normalized attention weights used to compute a weighted sum of frame features, followed by a fusion convolution.
    \item \textbf{Spectral consistency module}: Squeeze-and-Excitation style channel recalibration to preserve inter-band relationships after temporal fusion.
    \item \textbf{Progressive upsampler}: PixelShuffle $\times$2 followed by adaptive bilinear interpolation to the exact target resolution, with a physics-constrained sigmoid output head that clamps reflectances to the $[0,1]$ range.
\end{enumerate}

Two learning paradigms were evaluated:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Residual learning}: the network predicts a correction $\Delta$ added to a bilinear-interpolated baseline ($\hat{I}_\text{SR} = I_\text{bilinear} + \Delta$), providing a stable initialization.
    \item \textit{Direct learning}: the network maps LR frames directly to the full HR image without a baseline, relying on the full expressive capacity of the architecture.
\end{itemize}

A GAN training variant was also implemented for perceptual quality analysis. A scheduled perceptual loss (spectral angle loss weight linearly increasing from 0.005 to 0.05 over 40 warm-up epochs) was incorporated to balance pixel fidelity with spectral consistency. A 5-member heterogeneous ensemble (members differing in initialization seed) was trained to decompose predictive variance into aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, enabling principled uncertainty propagation in downstream data assimilation (Objective~2).

\subparagraph{SOTA comparison.}
The proposed model was benchmarked against two satellite-domain SR models evaluated with their original pretrained weights on a subset of the test set: OpenSR-Model \citep{donike2025} and SEN2SR \citep{aybar2025}, following the inter-comparative evaluation protocol of Aybar et~al.\ (2024). Both SOTA models were trained on Sentinel-2 / SPOT or Sentinel-2 / NAIP pairs and perform 4$\times$ upsampling; they were applied here without retraining. Both models scored at or below the bilinear baseline (Table~\ref{tab:sr_results}), reflecting the domain gap between their training data and the STA wetland environment, and demonstrating the necessity of domain-specific training data.

\paragraph{\textbf{Stage 2 — Transfer Learning to Very-High-Resolution Targets (Planned)}}

Stage~1 trained the EMO-TSR model on Sentinel-2 (LR) $\rightarrow$ PlanetScope (HR) pairs, learning to super-resolve 10~m and 20~m inputs to 3~m at a ${\approx}3.33\times$ scale factor. In Stage~2, we plan to leverage transfer learning to extend the model to scarcer very-high-resolution (VHR) targets such as WorldView-2/3 ($\sim$0.3--0.5~m panchromatic, $\sim$1.2--1.8~m multispectral) and ortho-rectified UAV imagery ($\sim$0.05--0.5~m). The key challenge is that VHR acquisitions over the STAs are sparse (typically fewer than 10 cloud-free scenes per year), which makes training a model from scratch infeasible and motivates a fine-tuning strategy.

The EMO-TSR model was trained at ${\approx}3.33\times$; reusing the pretrained encoder and temporal attention weights should be feasible for moderate factor shifts, but factors above ${\approx}6\times$ push beyond the spatial-frequency content learned by the decoder. Therefore, the most practical fine-tuning paths are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PlanetScope $\rightarrow$ WorldView MS ($2.5\times$)}: The closest to the original $3.33\times$ factor. The progressive upsampler can be reconfigured from $3.33\times$ to $2.5\times$ with minimal change (replacing the adaptive interpolation output target size while retaining the PixelShuffle $\times$2 step). We expect the encoder features to transfer well because the LR input (PlanetScope 3~m) already shares the domain learned in Stage~1.
    \item \textbf{Stage~1 SR $\rightarrow$ WorldView MS ($2.5\times$)}: The LR input is the Stage~1 super-resolved 3~m image. This creates a cascaded SR configuration: Sentinel-2 $\xrightarrow{3.33\times}$ SR 3~m $\xrightarrow{2.5\times}$ WV 1.2~m. This scenario is useful when no PlanetScope scene is available on a given date, but carries a risk of error accumulation from Stage~1 artifacts.
    \item \textbf{PlanetScope $\rightarrow$ UAV ($6\times$)}: The largest single-step factor amenable to fine-tuning. The progressive upsampler would require an additional PixelShuffle $\times$2 block (overall $4\times$ learned, plus $1.5\times$ adaptive interpolation), and the encoder would need to handle a substantially different spatial-frequency profile.
    \item \textbf{Sentinel-2 $\rightarrow$ WorldView ($8.33$--$16.67\times$)}: These factors are too large for single-step SR and would require either a cascaded two-stage approach (Stage~1 followed by Stage~2 fine-tuned model) or a purpose-built architecture with deeper decoder and progressive multi-step upsampling.
\end{itemize}

Key spectral considerations for transfer learning:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Common-subset strategy}: Five bands (Blue, Green, Red, Red~Edge, NIR-1) have close spectral counterparts across all three sensors. Fine-tuning using only these bands allows the pretrained encoder, temporal attention, and spectral consistency modules to be reused with minimal retraining; only the first convolutional stem and the output head need channel-count adjustment if the band count or ordering changes.
    
    \item \textbf{Unique WorldView bands}: WorldView provides Coastal Blue, Yellow, and NIR-2 bands that have no PlanetScope or Sentinel-2 counterpart in the Stage~1 training set. These bands cannot be generated by the pretrained decoder because it has never seen their spectral distribution. Two strategies are available: (a)~exclude these bands from fine-tuning and produce outputs only for the common-subset bands; (b)~add new output channels to the decoder head, initialize randomly, and train with a larger learning rate on the new channels while applying a lower learning rate to the pretrained backbone (differential learning rates).
    
    \item \textbf{Spectral harmonization direction}: In Stage~1, PlanetScope was harmonized \emph{towards} Sentinel-2 via histogram matching. For Stage~2, WorldView reflectances should be harmonized towards the same Sentinel-2 reference distribution used in Stage~1 so that the pretrained features encounter consistent input statistics. This can be achieved by applying the same band-wise CDF-matching procedure from Stage~1, using a Sentinel-2 scene acquired on the same date as the calibration reference. Alternatively, a linear regression model based on pseudo-invariant features (PIFs) can be used as a physics-grounded complementary normalization for the narrower WorldView bands where histogram matching may introduce bias due to the SRF difference.
    
    \item \textbf{Radiometric depth mismatch}: PlanetScope SuperDove scenes are delivered as 16-bit unsigned integers scaled to surface reflectance ($\times 10{,}000$); Sentinel-2 L2A uses the same convention. WorldView products, however, may be provided as top-of-atmosphere (TOA) radiance or as ortho-ready products that require an atmospheric correction step (e.g., FLAASH, ATCOR, or sensor-specific calibration coefficients) before they are comparable. All WorldView imagery must be atmospherically corrected to surface reflectance and scaled to the same $[0,1]$ normalized range used in Stage~1 before fine-tuning or inference.
\end{enumerate}

\subparagraph{Data requirements and fine-tuning strategy.}
WorldView scenes over the STAs will be limited (typically 5--15 cloud-free multispectral acquisitions over the study period). The critical question is how much VHR data is required for effective transfer.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tile yield per scene}: A single WorldView multispectral scene over one STA flow-way at 1.2~m covers ${\approx}3{,}000 \times 3{,}000$ pixels per km$^2$. Tiling at $256 \times 256$ (as in Stage~1) produces ${\approx}140$ non-overlapping tiles per km$^2$. A typical STA flow-way spans 5--20~km$^2$, yielding 700--2,800 tiles per cloud-free scene. With 5--15 usable scenes, the total fine-tuning set would comprise ${\approx}3{,}500$--$42{,}000$ tiles, or ${\approx}1{,}200$--$14{,}000$ temporal triplets (assuming three-frame sequences can be formed from temporally proximate scenes or by mixing with co-temporal Sentinel-2/PlanetScope LR inputs).

    \item \textbf{Minimum sample guidance}: Empirical evidence from satellite SR transfer-learning studies \citep{shermeyer2019} suggests that fine-tuning with as few as 500--1,000 paired tiles can yield $>$80\% of the fully retrained model's performance when the pretrained weights provide a strong prior. In our case, the Stage~1 encoder has already learned spatial features (edges, vegetation texture, water-boundary gradients) from 6,839 sequences covering the same geographic domain, so we expect the domain gap to be primarily spectral and resolution-related rather than content-related. We hypothesize that 3--5 cloud-free WorldView scenes per STA ($\approx$4{,}000--10{,}000 tiles) will be sufficient for effective fine-tuning.
    
    \item \textbf{Freezing strategy}: Transfer learning will proceed in two phases: (a)~\textit{head-only tuning} (5--10 epochs): freeze the encoder, temporal attention, and spectral consistency modules; train only the progressive upsampler and output head with the new scale factor and output channels, using a learning rate of $10^{-3}$; (b)~\textit{full fine-tuning} (20--40 epochs): unfreeze all layers and train end-to-end with a reduced learning rate ($10^{-5}$), applying a $10\times$ multiplier on the new/modified layers. Weight decay ($5 \times 10^{-6}$) and the same scheduled spectral angle loss from Stage~1 will be retained.
    
    \item \textbf{Temporal input adaptation}: WorldView scenes are not acquired systematically on a 5-day repeat cycle (unlike Sentinel-2). Forming three-frame temporal sequences from WorldView alone may be impossible. Two alternatives will be explored: (a)~\textit{mixed-sensor temporal stacks}: use the co-temporal Sentinel-2 and/or PlanetScope images as the auxiliary temporal frames, with only the center frame replaced by the WorldView-paired LR input—this requires spectral harmonization of all frames to a common reference; (b)~\textit{single-frame mode}: bypass the temporal attention module entirely and fine-tune the spatial encoder and decoder alone, forgoing the multi-temporal benefit.
    
    \item \textbf{Validation protocol}: Given the small fine-tuning set, a 5-fold cross-validation strategy (stratified by scene date) will be used instead of a single train/test split. Metrics will be reported as the mean $\pm$ standard deviation across folds. In addition, a ``zero-shot'' evaluation—applying the Stage~1 model to WorldView pairs without any fine-tuning after spectral harmonization only—will establish a lower-bound baseline and quantify how much of the performance gap is closed by fine-tuning.
\end{enumerate}

\subparagraph{Architectural modifications for Stage~2.}
The following modifications to the EMO-TSR architecture will be required:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input stem}: Replace the first depthwise separable convolution from $C_\text{in}=3$ (Stage~1, 10~m RGB) to $C_\text{in}=N$, where $N$ is the number of spectrally compatible input bands selected for the LR source. If PlanetScope is the LR input, $N=3$ or $5$ (same as Stage~1); if Sentinel-2 is used, $N$ remains 3 for the 10~m branch and 2 for the 20~m branch, and no modification is needed.
    
    \item \textbf{Output head}: Replace the final convolutional layer from $C_\text{out}=3$ (10~m) / $C_\text{out}=2$ (20~m) to the number of output bands in the VHR target. For WorldView common-subset fine-tuning, $C_\text{out}=5$ (Blue, Green, Red, Red~Edge, NIR-1). New output channels will be initialized with Kaiming normal weights.
    
    \item \textbf{Progressive upsampler}: Adjust the target output spatial dimensions in the adaptive interpolation layer. For a $2.5\times$ factor (PlanetScope $\rightarrow$ WV~MS), the PixelShuffle $\times$2 step produces $154 \times 154$ from $77 \times 77$; the adaptive interpolation then resizes to $192 \times 192$ ($=77 \times 2.5$, rounded). For a $6\times$ factor (PlanetScope $\rightarrow$ UAV), an additional PixelShuffle $\times$2 block is inserted (producing $308 \times 308$), followed by adaptive interpolation to $462 \times 462$ ($=77 \times 6$). The new PixelShuffle block is randomly initialized; the existing block's weights are copied from Stage~1.
    
    \item \textbf{Physics-constrained head}: The sigmoid output bounds ($[0,1]$ reflectance range) will be retained for WorldView surface-reflectance products. If UAV data use uncalibrated DN values, the output will be switched to a ReLU activation with dataset-specific min/max normalization.
\end{enumerate}

\subparagraph{Expected outcomes for Stage~2.}
This stage will produce: (a)~a fine-tuned EMO-TSR model capable of generating ${\approx}1.2$~m multispectral SR imagery from Sentinel-2 or PlanetScope inputs over the STAs; (b)~an empirical transfer-learning curve quantifying SR performance (PSNR, SSIM, RMSE, ERGAS, SID) as a function of the number of WorldView training scenes; (c)~an analysis of the benefit of spectral harmonization pre-processing and temporal-input mixing for low-data fine-tuning; and (d)~uncertainty estimates from the ensemble for the new SR product.

\subsubsection{Proposed Methodology}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Identify model variables (BGC and CASM), and their corresponding mathematical formulations, that can be mapped from remote sensing products.
    \item Identify suitable remote sensing products that have been used to map those state variables (e.g., carbon biomass of emergent vegetation to optical or microwave spectral data).
    \item Train multi-temporal models to map state variables to remote sensing data using the time series imagery derived from Objective 1.     \item Use the best-performing model to produce an estimation of the state variables' values for the area and time of interest.
    \item Aggregate the observed pixel values into segments. The magnitude of these segments and the aggregation method are still to be defined. This will be done as the difference between the values estimated by the model and the observed value from remote sensing features, multiplied by a trust value. The trust value will be obtained as a combination of the remote sensing model's uncertainty and the quality of the input image data.
\end{itemize}

% Causal learning algorithms for time series can help clarify the partially resolved question of which key drivers shape P dynamics in the STAs by disentangling causality from correlation. However, caution is necessary because the validity of these algorithms depends on specifying a set of assumptions about both the data and the system \citep{causalrunge2018}. To expand their domain of applicability, numerous causal learning methods have been developed, yet ensuring reliable outcomes in natural systems such as the STAs remains difficult due to limited ground truth data and the sensitivity of these methods to assumption validity. Practical constraints further complicate validating assumptions such as the presence of dynamical noise, hidden confounders, and sufficient sampling rates. For instance, convergent cross mapping (CCM) performs best (i.e., maximizes true positives and minimizes false positives) when around 20\% of the time series variance arises from dynamical noise; any deviation from this can substantially increase false positive or decrease true positive rates \citep{causalrunge2018}. Consequently, exploring multiple algorithms with different assumptions and comparing their outputs against domain knowledge is essential for achieving robust inferences in the STAs context.

Similarly, \citet{nonlinearmedina2019} employed state-space modeling to map causal interactions among the deterministic component of 19 covariates and TP and then developed data-driven models using these outcomes. The outcomes of these studies offer valuable, but distinct, benefits for understanding the complex dynamics of STAs.

% The first framework to appear in causal discovery for time series analysis was Granger causality (GC) \citep{GrangerCausality}. To report causality, GC methods test whether adding past values from an external vector Y improves the prediction of current values of X \citep{causalmoraffah2021}. GC methods have been applied in various fields, including earth science studies \citep{grangertriacca2005,memorymcgraw2018}; however, many GC methods have been proven to have multiple limitations with sub-sampled time series, high dimensionality, stochasticity, dynamic lags, instantaneous effects, latent confounders \citep{inferringrunge2019,discoveringvarambally2023}. Numerous modifications of the original GC-based models have been proposed over the years to address these limitations. \citet{causalmoraffah2021} presents a comprehensive review of these methods, the Variable-lag GC by \citet{variablelagamornbunchornvej2019} the most recent of them. This method uses dynamic time wrapping (DTW) to capture dynamic time-delayed effects.  

% An alternative approach to GC is conditional independence-based algorithms. These methods are based on the time order assumption and the Causal Markov, Faithfulness, and Sufficiency assumption \citep{causalmoraffah2021}. Transfer entropy is a conditional independence-based algorithm that uses an information theoretic approach. Modern versions of this method have been designed to improve the capacity of this algorithm to handle high-dimensionality and to recognize other than just pairwise causal direction. For instance, \citet{causalityzhou2022} presents a matrix-based transfer entropy algorithm that can detect direct and indirect causation in high-dimensional datasets. Another approach that uses conditional independence is the PC algorithm and its many modifications. These methods are widely used because of their flexibility in terms of data distributions and causal relationships. One of the most recent and relevant modifications was proposed by \citet{detectingrunge2017}. The Momentary Conditional Independence PC algorithm (PCMCI) is a two-step algorithm specially designed for time-series analysis that avoids detecting spurious links that come from the violation of the independently and identically distributed assumption. This algorithm has been successfully implemented in various environmental and climate applications \citep{detectingrunge2019, discoveringrunge2020}. The authors of this algorithm claimed that it could handle high-dimensional, linear, and nonlinear data sets with state-of-the-art detection power. This algorithm is best suited for stochastic systems.

% Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) in the causal inference domain relies on qualitative causal assumptions, observational data, and specific queries to produce causal claims. The validity of these claims is limited to the domain defined by the causal assumptions derived from domain knowledge \citep{causalpearl2012}. SEM approaches are considered powerful methods that can identify causal interactions even when two variables are contemporary, and that can handle latent confounders \citep{inferringrunge2019,discoveringvarambally2023}. The Linear Non-Gaussian Acyclic Model (LiNGAM) \citep{linearshimizu2006} and its adaptation to handle autoregressive data,\citep{estimationhyvrinen2010}, high dimensionality \citep{multidimensionalschaechtle2013} are among the most widely used SEM approaches to estimate causal structures; however, these methods are not well suited to handle history-dependent noise issues (i.e., the change of noise distribution due to past actions) \citep{discoveringvarambally2023}. One of the latest developments in SEM methods is the Mixed Causal Discovery (MCD) approach developed by \citet{discoveringvarambally2023}. MCD assumes that the data are not generated by only one but for multiple processes. It uses a mixture of causal models to represent each of these processes and then combines them into one to provide the causal structure of the covariates under study. This method can be set to handle linear and nonlinear processes and history-dependent noise issues. Few applications of SEM methods have been made in earth and environmental sciences. \citet{HU2024107130} used SEM to study the covariates that influenced P retention and flow in the STAs in Florida. \citet{causalperezsuay2020} explored a structural causal modeling approach for non-deterministic systems in geosciences. \citet{causalityotgonbaatar2022} used SEM to study the causal relationship between multiple satellite imagery.
